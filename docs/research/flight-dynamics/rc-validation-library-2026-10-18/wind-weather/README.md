Research acquisition: **2026-10-19 UTC**. This collection supports source-grounded comparisons for the existing [wind track](../../../../WIND-PLAN.md), especially spatial fields, shear, distributed aircraft response and future terrain/thermal work. It collects external evidence; no simulator parameters or runtime behavior were changed. The [source manifest](sources.json) is authoritative for source identity, originals, access, inspection depth and limitations. New files comprise 26 PDFs, two NetCDFs, one CSV, one ZIP, two HTML snapshots and four terrain/statistics text files. ## Reading order and comparison targets

| Question | Start here | Match before comparing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How should a thermal vary with height, radius and wind? | Thermal model and aircraft/atmospheric observations below | Boundary-layer height, surface heating, background wind, drift, sampling and aircraft sink-rate inference |
| What changes near the ground or across a wing? | Low-altitude gust/sonic measurements and spanwise studies | Measurement height, roughness, stability, aircraft size/speed, spatial separation, axes and spectrum convention |
| How do vortex or downdraft encounters differ? | Earth dust-devil, microburst and rotor studies | Phenomenon, geometry, core/measurement height, observation cadence and measured versus inferred velocity |
| How does terrain change the field? | Askervein benchmark, Perdigão and MATERHORN | Terrain dimensions, wind direction, AGL/MSL, stratification, roughness and sensor placement |
| Which numeric evidence is actually local? | Individual manifest `local_path` and data notes | Full original versus selected member/preview, units, clocks, missing markers, averaging and QC |

The relevant phenomena are different. A thermal is buoyant rising flow; a dust devil adds a rotating core; hill/forest wakes involve drag and separation; mountain rotors involve terrain and stratification; a microburst is a strong convective downdraft and outflow. A background vector plus independent random noise does not establish these structures. This distinction guides comparison choices, not a new implementation specification.

For any later simulator comparison, retain wind as air velocity with explicit axes and sample positions/times, and compare the observation operator as well as the physical model. Meteorological direction is “from”; transport vectors are “toward”; vertical velocity in an up-positive source changes sign in NED. Dissipation, image coordinates, inferred climb lift and commanded controls are not interchangeable wind measurements. Use the existing [flight logging workflow](../../../../FLIGHT-LOGGING.md) for a candidate run; software replay does not prove atmospheric realism.

## Thermal and convective-boundary-layer sources

This section separates **measured air motion**, **flight-derived lift proxies**, and **prescribed/simulated thermal fields**. A glider's climb rate is not ambient vertical wind: approximately, air vertical velocity equals aircraft climb rate plus its still-air sink rate, with sign and energy corrections depending on the measurement method. Likewise, a flight-derived drift estimate is not a direct wind-sensor reading.

### Direct thermal structure and boundary-layer measurements

- [Lenschow & Stephens (1980), “The Role of Thermals in the Convective Boundary Layer”](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00122351) analyze NCAR Electra transects during AMTEX. They identify thermal segments from humidity anomalies over 5-minute, 30-km legs after a 5-km high-pass cutoff; humidity is preferred because thermal air may be cooler than its surroundings near the top of the mixed layer. Their mixed-layer analysis reports height-dependent thermal length/count scaling and finds pressure/edge effects and vertical-velocity-variance divergence relevant to the mean updraft balance. This is statistical aircraft evidence, not a ready-made isolated thermal map. The public publisher page was accessible, but its PDF endpoint returned the article page during retrieval, so no local copy is recorded.
- [Young (1988), “Turbulence Structure of the Convective Boundary Layer. Part II: Phoenix 78 Aircraft Observations of Thermals and Their Environment”](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0469%281988%29045%3C0727%3Atsotcb%3E2.0.co%3B2) applies conditional sampling to aircraft vertical-velocity and temperature spectra to report thermal size, spacing, and structure. The Penn State institutional record exposes the abstract; the AMS PDF endpoint denied retrieval, so the record has no local path.
- [Young (1988), Part III: “The Vertical Velocity Budgets of Thermals and Their Environment”](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0469%281988%29045%3C2039%3Atsotcb%3E2.0.co%3B2) uses Phoenix 78 observations to analyze separate budgets for thermal updrafts and compensating environmental downdrafts, and reports qualitatively different forcing of the two branches. This is a particularly useful direct field reference for not modeling thermals as an isolated updraft. The Penn State institutional record exposes the abstract; no local PDF was obtained.
- [Hardt, Schäfer & Rohde-Brandenburger (2023), DLR glider transects](https://elib.dlr.de/210473/) report a miniaturized chilled-mirror hygrometer with up-to-10-Hz humidity response installed in the DLR Discus-2c, and 26 thermal cross-sections on three summer-2022 days at different heights, flown both along and across wind. The available one-page conference abstract describes measurements of width, vertical velocity, temperature, and humidity differences but gives no numeric tables; DLR marks it non-refereed. Its PDF is saved at `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/hardt-2023-dlr-thermal-transects.pdf`.
- [de Boer et al. (2022), RAAVEN during ATOMIC](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-14-19-2022) document 39 flights and over 77 flight hours by a fixed-wing, RC-derived small UAS near Barbados. Profiles and level legs sampled the marine lower atmosphere from about 20 m to 1 km; the instrument suite reports kinematic and thermodynamic state. This is useful small-aircraft instrumentation and environmental variability, but it is a tropical trade-wind boundary-layer campaign rather than isolated soaring thermals. The public NOAA archive contains 39 NetCDF files totaling 703 MB ([dataset DOI](https://doi.org/10.25921/jhnd-8e58)); one 18,927,992-byte file, `ATOMIC_CU-RAAVEN_20200127_134726_B1.nc`, is saved at `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/atomic-raaven-20200127-134726-b1.nc`. Its `time_10hz` dimension has 84,480 records and metadata describes 10-Hz offsets; attitude, flight-state, and quality flags are present, so records are not assumed to be airborne/valid. The NetCDF classic file's global `Creation_Date` is 09-Apr-2021. NCEI's landing page lists archive versions 1.1 and 2.2, but the exact version mapping for this unversioned directory file was not established; its download URL and checksum pin the acquired bytes. The file was inspected read-only for metadata; no samples or QC were recomputed. Article PDF: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/raaven-2022-atomic-uav-measurements.pdf`.
- [Lappin et al. (2024), TRACER small-UAS data](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-16-2525-2024) describe RAAVEN and CopterSonde observations during sea-breeze events, before/near convection, and quiescent periods. The campaign collected over 200 flight hours across 61 days, sampling profiles and stepped levels from roughly 20 to 609 m AGL. These are direct small-UAS wind/thermodynamic field observations at useful near-ground scales; they document boundary-layer evolution and local circulations, not sampled thermal-core maps. ARM NetCDF access requires a free account; the public paper is saved at `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/tracer-2024-uas-boundary-layer-data.pdf`.

### Flight-derived proxies and field soaring evidence

- [Hernández-Aguayo, Cristelli & Benzaquen (2026), “From Flight Logs to Atmospheric Science”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00241) is a preprint analyzing 110,730 French paraglider flights (2017–2024) and 1.47 million climb segments. It reports approximately 1-Hz GNSS position/altitude and uses climb rate as a thermal-strength proxy; reported mean rates are about 1.0 m/s on plains and 1.3 m/s in high mountains, with 1.0–1.5 m/s climb-rate variability. It couples these proxies to hourly 0.25° ERA5 reanalysis. These values are not raw vertical wind; sink-rate correction, pilot/conditions selection, and coarse gridded predictor scales matter. No standalone raw-log archive is identified in the paper. PDF: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/hernandez-aguayo-2026-paraglider-thermals.pdf`.
- [Allen & Lin (2007), CloudSwift guidance and control](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20070022339) report a 2005 research-motorglider campaign with 23 thermal encounters. Reused local source: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/sailplanes/allen-lin-2007-soaring-flight-control.pdf`. Its thermal estimates are flight-derived and the results concern closed-loop soaring, not independent atmospheric probes.
- [Reddy et al. (2018), glider soaring in the field](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0533-0) validate learned thermalling on a two-metre glider in real weather. Reused local source: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/sailplanes/reddy-2018-glider-field-learning.pdf`. The thermal/air-motion signals are inferred from glider state and control; no public raw log archive was located in the inspected article/thesis trail.
- [Tabor, Guilliard & Kolobov (2018), ArduSoar](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08215) report thermalling-controller flights with a Parkzone Radian Pro, the most directly RC-scale field-soaring source in this set. Reused local source: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/sailplanes/tabor-2018-ardusoar.pdf`. It evaluates control and thermal estimation, not independent meteorological truth.

### Prescribed or simulated thermal models

- [Allen (2006), NASA's analytical updraft model](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20060004052) derives statistical thermal scales from NOAA Desert Rock surface-radiation and rawinsonde observations. The model uses convective velocity scale w* (m/s) and mixing-layer depth zi (m) to generate thermal size, spacing, vertical-velocity profile, and maximum height; its representative cases span w*=0.46–5.02 m/s and mean zi=54–3647 m. It is a useful parameterized model family, not an observed universal thermal. The full report and code listing are in `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/allen-2006-analytical-updraft-model.pdf`; the code was not run.
- [Flato et al. (2024), vulture-inspired thermal soaring](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-48670-x) study a point-mass glider in simulation with a prescribed thermal (w*=5 m/s, z*=2000 m) and horizontal winds of 1, 3, and 5 m/s. The measured vulture trajectory is a behavior comparison, not a measurement of that simulated thermal or its wind field. Article: `references/rc-validation-library-2026-10-18/wind-weather/thermals/vulture-2024-windy-thermal-soaring.pdf`.

### Simulator comparison notes

These sources support comparison of (1) thermal width and vertical-velocity profile against direct transects, (2) height-dependent thermal statistics and entrainment against aircraft CBL studies, (3) a background/environmental return-motion budget rather than an isolated positive updraft, and (4) measured near-ground variability from small-UAS profiles. The mass-continuity implication in (3) is a simulator constraint/inference, not a numeric compensating-sink parameter supplied by these papers. Keep thermal size/strength tied to mixed-layer scales where those are measured or modeled, and label any wind, climb, or thermal-drift values by whether they were directly sensed, estimated from aircraft motion, or prescribed in simulation.

## Gusts, turbulence spectra, and spatial coherence

**Acquisition date: 2026-10-19 UTC.** This collection separates measured atmospheric flow from a turbulence generator and from UAV wind estimates. The three NASA reports already cited by [`wind-physics-primary-sources.md`](../../../wind-physics-primary-sources.md) were newly acquired as local originals here. The JSON catalogue has 11 entries; five newly acquired PDFs and one numeric dataset-page preview were verified, one existing AirSTAR NASA preprint was reused by path, and four sources remain metadata-only after normal public download attempts failed or exceeded the bounded data budget.

### Findings for the simulator

**Dryden and von Kármán are spectral choices, not RC weather presets.** Fichtl’s NASA TM-78141 develops a Dryden filter using component standard deviations `σ`, integral scales `L`, and nondimensional frequency `Ω = ωL/V`; the filter is a way to turn a chosen spectrum into time-varying noise, with vehicle true airspeed setting the time scale. It is an engineering model, not an atmospheric dataset. NASA’s low-altitude Beech D-18 data show why one spectrum should not be treated as universal: vertical gusts were distinctly non-Gaussian, low-frequency PSDs had peaks or dips, and Dryden fit some inertial-range points while von Kármán fit others. In the B-57B spanwise-gradient report, von Kármán spectra fit the reported 0.04–20 Hz range, while the authors also document the sensitivity of two-point estimates to truncation, aliasing and estimator bias. ([Fichtl, 1977](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19780004071); [Ganzer et al., 1977](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19770025195); [Frost et al., 1987](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19880000625))

**Wing gust gradients are measurable, but their scales must be transferred carefully.** The D-18 report measured all three gust components at both wing tips. The B-57B program placed probes at the nose and both tips, enabling simultaneous one- and two-point spectra, correlations and velocity differences. That evidence supports spatially sampling the field if the aerodynamic model needs asymmetric wing loading; it does not provide small-aircraft gradient parameters. The NASA studies use large instrumented airplanes, long flight records and conditions whose wavelengths and instrument geometry are unlike a 1–2 m RC wing. A small wing may see a nearly coherent gust for some scales and a strong gradient for others; the relevant dimensionless comparison is wing span against the local turbulence scale, which remains condition-dependent.

**Coherence depends on the measurement direction and conditions.** The Great Belt field report is a primary over-water coherence experiment, but its PDF endpoint returned 403 and no numerical result was extracted here. FINO1 analysis used three mast heights during neutral January 2008 conditions and found substantial differences between measured vertical coherence and standards-based model values; the Mann model trend was closer than the Kaimal-plus-coherence alternative for this subset. Neither result is a universal model for lateral coherence across an RC airplane. ([Mann et al., 1991](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/the-great-belt-coherence-experiment-a-study-of-atmospheric-turbul/); [Eliassen and Obhrai, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.egypro.2016.09.199))

**Near-surface flow has height-dependent, stability-dependent structure.** The Penn State Utqiagvik release offers five 50 Hz sonic levels from 0.5 to 7.5 m, with three velocity components and virtual temperature. This is unusually relevant to low-altitude gradients and anisotropy, but it samples strongly stable Arctic conditions and its full release is about 27 GB. It remains metadata-only. These statistics should not become default RC gust values without selecting a matching stability, surface, height and speed regime. ([Penn State Data Commons, dataset 10.26208/9QA7-1E73](https://www.datacommons.psu.edu/commonswizard/MetadataDisplay.aspx?Dataset=6506))

**A moving aircraft’s wind estimate is not a point sonic measurement.** BLUECAT5 and MASC-3 infer wind by combining airspeed/flow-angle probe readings with vehicle ground velocity and attitude; the MASC-3 PANGAEA preview is a small, numeric example of such a reconstructed three-component record. The sampled preview contains 2,000 rows from an approximately 55-minute flight record whose full PANGAEA file has 1,175,216 points. Its reported altitude is above the ellipsoid rather than AGL. Witte et al. compare a five-hole-probe system with a sonic mounted on a 7.62 m tower; probe bandwidth and navigation/yaw errors limit what the wind vector means. Rautenberg et al.’s abstract-level calibration results further warn that changes in UAS airspeed can leave mean winds comparatively robust while changing TKE, component variances, covariances and integral scales. These are research UAS systems, not RC flight validations. ([Witte et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos8100195); [PANGAEA flight record](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.946960); [Rautenberg et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos10030124))

A separate validation mode is available when wind is measured directly by a sonic on a moving platform: SAMURAI-S suspends a research-grade sonic under a multirotor and compares it with mast sonics. Its ten 10–28 minute samples show good horizontal-component agreement but a consistent vertical-fluctuation overestimate that grows with mean wind speed. The paper says supporting data can be requested from its authors, so it is evidence about sensor behavior, not a retained numeric wind tape. ([Ghirardelli et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.5194/amt-18-2103-2025))

**Time-to-space conversion is conditional.** Applying Taylor’s frozen-turbulence approximation (`distance ≈ mean advection speed × elapsed time`) to map a stationary sonic series into spatial length, or to interpret a flight time series as a spatial spectrum, assumes the sampled field is sufficiently stationary and advects past the sensor without evolving rapidly. Stability transitions, low advection speed, strong vertical shear and vehicle motion can break that approximation. This is an inference from the measurement designs and the explicit transition cases in the UAV studies, not a universal rejection of Taylor conversion. Preserve the native sampling frame and report speed, height, stability and processing method whenever spectra or integral scales are compared.

## Dust devils, atmospheric rotors and convective outflows

These sources describe different wind structures. A dust-filled rotating core, a mountain rotor and a thunderstorm-related surface outflow cannot be calibrated from the same generic swirling-noise field. The retained evidence includes in-situ Earth observations, a surface-network report, lidar reconstruction, and an image-derived event catalog. None is a complete instantaneous 3D atmospheric velocity field.

| Primary source | What was measured or derived | Useful comparison and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Tratt et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1029/2003JE002161), [coauthor PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/dcatling/Tratt_etal2003.pdf) | Instrumented vehicle encounters near Eloy, Arizona in June 2001; wind at 5 Hz approximately 3.5 m AGL, with temperature and pressure measurements. | Despite the Mars-oriented title, these are Earth observations. ### Smith Creek Valley: numerical observation-instance table

The official ZIP contains `SuppFile.pdf` and `TableS1.csv`. Both members were also retained individually for direct access. ZIP CRC passed; the extracted PDF and CSV are byte-identical to the corresponding archive members. They are **three companion artifacts from the same study**, not independent datasets or campaigns. The paper, ZIP, PDF and CSV have their own manifest records and share a study identity.

`TableS1.csv` has **1,786 data rows and 44 consistently populated column positions**. Fields include `datetime`, frame/sequence/instance IDs, projected coordinates, image coordinates, duration, derived width/dustiness descriptors and quality fields. Individual cells can be blank: “44 columns” does not mean every quantity was observed for every instance.

The camera cadence is 20 s, and multiple rows can refer to the same time or tracked dust devil. The table has 296 unique timestamp strings; neither that number nor the 1,786 row count is the number of independent vortices. `u1/v1` through `u4/v4` and the later image-center fields are image coordinates, **not wind components**. Width and location depend on image/geometric reconstruction and quality codes; use the supplementary definitions before selecting or comparing rows. There is no continuous wind or pressure log in this package.

### ISFS data interpretation and an unacquired numeric lead

The retained [Perdigão ISFS QC report](https://www.eol.ucar.edu/content/perdig%C3%A3o-isfs-data-report) is unchanged HTML describing calibration, sonic orientation/tilt and processing caveats. ### LAPSE-RATE: acquired time–height turbulence estimates

The [2020 campaign overview](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-12-3357-2020) describes coordinated small-UAS, surface and remote observations of morning transitions, drainage flows, convection and turbulence in Colorado. The [2021 lidar paper](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-13-3539-2021) provides the estimation method and instrument-comparison limits. Both complete PDFs were acquired.

One complete file, `UCB.DPLR1.c1.20180713.233910.nc`, was acquired from [Zenodo record 4399967](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4399967). This is the **WC68/Saguache file**, one of three files in that release; the other WindCube and 1.70 GB Halo files were not downloaded. Original bytes match the repository's published MD5 and size, and SHA-256 is retained.

Read-only inspection of the NetCDF4/HDF5 arrays established:

- 154,407 epoch-second entries, with first/last entries at 2018-07-13 23:39:10.379983 UTC and 2018-07-20 16:13:58.350016 UTC.
- Ten 20-m-deep measurement-volume heights from 40 to 220 m; site elevation is separately reported as 2,388 m MSL.
- `epsilon` and `epsilon_30min`, each shaped **10 × 154,407**, in **m²/s³**. These are estimated turbulence dissipation and its averaged product, not three-component wind velocity.
- The documented missing marker is finite `-9999.9`. It occupies 631,434 instantaneous-product cells and 399,080 averaged-product cells out of 1,544,070 each. Finiteness alone would miss these gaps.
- Sampling is irregular: median adjacent interval is approximately 3.7 s, with one backward step of approximately 7.7 s. Keep original ordering and establish a policy before any time-based comparison; no samples were reordered, interpolated or replaced here.

The field label “instantaneous” does not remove the retrieval's finite windows, volume or spectral assumptions. This numeric product can constrain time/height variation of turbulence statistics after a measurement-matched comparison. It cannot be used directly as a velocity disturbance or a complete 3D gust tape. Data were opened with the existing free h5py reader pinned to 3.14.0 in ignored scratch space; no project dependency or runtime physics changed.

## Acquisition and verification boundary

Only public publisher, author, institutional and original repository endpoints were used. Failed, login-required or oversized leads remain explicitly unacquired; no third parties were contacted. Numeric products are labeled by processing and retained scope. A paper plot, abstract or metadata record does not become a raw log because it has a catalogue entry.

Original file sizes and SHA-256, PDF signatures/parsing and ZIP CRCs are checked by [the existing catalogue verifier](../../../../../research/reference-library/catalog.py). Additional data-specific inspection is recorded per source: CSV/HTML table structure, published archive hashes/member identity, or read-only array decoding. Source code and downloaded research scripts were not executed; published fits and retrieval algorithms were not reproduced. Public access does not establish redistribution rights; the manifest preserves license and version limits.
