# AV-17-R1 manufacturer and handling source audit

2026-10-14 · Research only · SebArt Avanti S Jet A200, FAA/NASA/NACA aerodynamic references

## Manufacturer evidence

The primary source is SebArt's [Avanti S Jet 2.2m assembly manual](https://www.sebart.it/download/AVANTI%20S%20JET%202.2m-Manual%20Intro.pdf). It identifies the full-composite-fuselage, wood-wing-and-stabilizer A200 as the Avanti S Jet 2.2m ARF (p. 1). The manual's actual dimensions are 200 cm wingspan and 222 cm length, with 10.5 kg RTF dry mass using a P100 turbine (p. 1). SebArt's [Avanti S Jet product page](https://www.sebart.it/jets-L.html) repeats 200 cm span, 222 cm length, and 10.5 kg dry. Thus “2.2m” is the model name/length class in this manual, not its span. SebArt's 2026 [jet price list](https://sebart.it/download/sebart-pricelist.pdf) now labels the A200 “Avanti S - 2.3m”; the sources do not explain that naming change or establish whether it denotes a revision. Keep the manual's A200 specification distinct from later listings until a build/revision is matched.

The manual describes the aircraft as a light-wing-loading aerobatic-trainer/3D compromise, based on the Hawk and modified with pattern-model aerodynamic ideas (p. 1), and explicitly says it is not a pylon racer or speed jet (p. 3). It warns that the light structure and very large control surfaces make high speed inadvisable; the plane had been tested to 250 km/h and SebArt did not recommend exceeding that speed due to flutter or structural failure (p. 3). These are manufacturer claims and operating guidance, not a formal structural envelope; the manual gives no test conditions or load data. It recommends a P100 for jet beginners, P140/P160 for FAI F3S, and P180 or more for 3D (p. 2).

| Manual recommendation | High-rate surface travel | Normal / pattern | Start / landing | 3D |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Elevator | ±30° | D/R 50%, expo 40% | D/R 100%, expo 80% | D/R 100%, expo 60% |
| Aileron | 30° up / 25° down | D/R 50%, expo 20% | D/R 100%, expo 60% | D/R 100%, expo 40% |
| Rudder | ±30° | D/R 80%, expo 60% | D/R 100%, expo 80% | D/R 100%, expo 60% |

These values are SebArt's p. 4 recommendations. Expo sign is positive for JR and negative for Futaba radios (p. 5); the manual does not define a radio-independent expo curve. Flaps are full up for normal/pattern, 20° down for start with 8% elevator-down mix, and 50° down for landing with 20% elevator-down mix (p. 4). The recommended CG is 250 mm behind the wing leading edge at the fuselage; 240 mm is identified as good for jet beginners and 260 mm for unlimited 3D (p. 5). No fuel-state qualifier accompanies these CG figures.

The A200 manual and product page do not publish wing area, numerical wing loading, a 1-g stall speed, CLmax, critical AoA, or stall progression. The dry mass is the only manufacturer mass figure found; it is not an all-up/fuelled mass. The current simulator uses a 0.7023 m² wing-area estimate from its AV-02 geometry blockout, not a SebArt drawing or measurement. On that area, the manufacturer's 10.5 kg dry mass implies 14.95 kg/m² (149.5 g/dm²), while the AV-06 half-tank simulation case of 11.705 kg implies 16.67 kg/m² (166.7 g/dm²). Both are conditional calculations; neither is a manufacturer-reported wing loading. AV-06's 3.2 L tank and fuel loading are estimated inputs, and the manual does not state the CG fuel condition.

The A200 must not be confused with SebArt's separately documented Mini Avanti foam EDF models: the official [older models page](https://www.sebart.it/old_models.html) lists the 80 mm EPO foam Mini Avanti S at 1236 mm span and 1300 mm length, while SebArt's [Mini Avanti jet introduction](https://www.sebart.it/download/2015-manuali%20MiniAvanti/Mini%20Avanti%20JET%20ARF%20manual%20intro.pdf) describes a different 1360 mm fiberglass/wood EDF-or-P20 model. Neither is the A200.

## Aerodynamic evidence and relevance

- The FAA [Airplane Flying Handbook, FAA-H-8083-3C, Chapter 5](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/06_afh_ch5.pdf) says a stall occurs when critical AoA is exceeded, not because airspeed alone is too low; increased load factor raises the stall speed. A 2-g condition raises it by about 41% (pp. 5-9 to 5-10). Its “Accelerated Stalls” section names pullouts from steep dives as a common inadvertent trigger and says abrupt-maneuver stalls tend to be more aggressive (pp. 5-19 to 5-20). The recovery discussion prioritizes reducing AoA; then level the wings, coordinate, and use power as needed (p. 5-15). This directly explains how an aircraft can stall while fast during a pullout.
- Using AV-06's estimated 17.7 m/s clean 1-g stall speed only as an illustration, the square-root load-factor relation gives about 25.0 m/s at 2 g and 30.7 m/s at 3 g. These are consequences of the simulator's estimated mass/area/CLmax, not A200 flight-test values. A useful handling check should record AoA and normal load through the pull, rather than classify a stall from speed or pitch attitude alone.
- The FAA handbook explains that wing twist is commonly used to make the root stall before the tip and preserve some aileron effectiveness; it also warns that downward aileron on an already stalled wing can worsen local AoA and stall progression (p. 5-15). The historical NACA [Report 796, “An Interim Report on the Stability and Control of Tailless Airplanes”](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20050241739/downloads/20050241739.pdf) reports the opposite tendency for swept-back planforms: tip stall can occur first, over the aileron region, with rolling and pitching instability (PDF pp. 2-3; report pp. 421-422). These sources show that stall progression depends on twist, section and planform. They do not identify the Avanti's section or twist. AV-06's tip-first incidence/load distribution is explicitly estimated, not manufacturer data.
- NASA's [CR-1999-209547, “Modeling of Longitudinal Unsteady Aerodynamics of a Wing-Tail Combination”](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/19990100653.pdf) treats tail downwash as unsteady: it compares lag-in-downwash with wing-circulation growth and delayed tail lift, finding their predictions diverge more for long-tail aircraft in low-speed rapid maneuvers (abstract; NTRS record [here](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19990100653)). This supports making tail flow responsive to pitch transients where evidence permits, but supplies no A200 coefficient or sign/magnitude for its pullout response.
- NASA's [CR-2462, “Analysis of Dynamic Stall Using Unsteady Boundary-Layer Theory”](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/19740026587.pdf) finds pitch rate affects separation/reversal timing for a pitching airfoil. It supports distinguishing a rate-dependent stall transient from a static lift curve, but is not a matched low-speed A200 test. Do not tune an Avanti dynamic-stall delay from this report alone.

## Implications for the simulator

The strongest immediate hypothesis is an accelerated stall caused by control rate and pullout load: if the normal/pattern mode commands the full manual ±30° elevator travel, it bypasses the manual's 50% D/R setting (nominally ±15° if radio scaling is linear) before expo. The simulator data contains the manual high-rate throw plus D/R/expo inputs, but this audit does not verify how the runtime applies them. Confirm actual commanded surface angle by mode and speed before changing stall coefficients. Dynamic pressure makes a given surface deflection produce stronger aerodynamic force at higher speed, so a full-rate pull at dive speed can raise load and AoA sharply.

The sources do not justify changing the Avanti's stall AoA, CLmax, tip/root sequence, or tail-effectiveness schedule to match a video or an unverified anecdote. The unresolved physical inputs are the matched A200 revision, planform/wing area, section and twist, actual control linkage travel and radio mode, measured CG at known fuel mass, and stall/pullout observations. AV-06 labels its 0.7023 m² area, CLmax 0.85, stall-blend width, tip incidence, tail pressure ratio and post-stall wing/tail laws as estimates. The current model's 1-g stall is therefore a scenario prediction, not a known airplane limit.
