# Ugly Stik: primary sources and a validation program

Research synthesis for source baseline `ad21070798bfb9658785576a9c0b8bfc9a71fc7f`; sources accessed 2026-11-15. The two stock configurations are different experimental objects: the basic Jensen Stik uses a prescribed-RPM glow representation; the 6S conversion uses a different motor, propeller and battery installation. The external references below do not establish the correct behavior of either particular simulated aircraft. Their main value is to design measurements that can discriminate between control-path, propulsion and aerodynamic causes of an unsatisfactory feel.

The immediate principle is to **measure the specific assembled aircraft and the full command chain first**, preserve an unchanged simulator baseline, and judge candidates against measurements and separate, preselected flight maneuvers. Do not choose new coefficients solely to match another aircraft, one maneuver or a pilot's first impression. A candidate may pass all software checks while remaining physically unvalidated.

## What the primary sources can support

**Electric propeller measurements.** Dantsker, Caccamo, Deters and Selig, [*Performance Testing of APC Electric Fixed-Blade UAV Propellers*](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/pubs/DantskerCaccamoDetersSelig-2022-AIAA-Paper-2022-4020-APCEPropTestsVol4.pdf), AIAA 2022-4020 (2022), and the associated [UIUC Propeller Database, Volume 4](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/props/volume-4/propDB-volume-4.html), report wind-tunnel and static measurements for APC Thin Electric propellers. This includes the **12×8E propeller type specified by the electric Stik**, which makes it a more directly applicable component reference than a different diameter or pitch. It is still not a measurement of the user's individual propeller, complete motor/ESC installation, or the aircraft in flight. UIUC says its coefficients use actual measured propeller diameter, which can differ from the nominal size.

The [12×8E, 6,024 RPM advancing-flow run](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/props/volume-4/data/apce_12x8_0628od_6024.txt) covers approximately `J=0.334–0.792`; for example, at `J=0.493073` its measured `CT=0.074640` and `CP=0.047084`. The separate [12×8E static dataset](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/props/volume-4/data/apce_12x8_static_0621od.txt) spans about 1,027–7,500 RPM. Here `J=V/(nD)`, `CT=T/(ρn²D⁴)` and `CP=P/(ρn³D⁵)`, where `n` is revolutions per second. Carry the measured-diameter convention, actual RPM, density, flow speed and uncertainty into any comparison. The 6,024-RPM data do not directly validate 10,000-RPM performance, and a positive-RPM wind-tunnel sweep cannot establish the stopped-propeller or zero-RPM limit.

**Manufacturer propeller predictions and limits.** APC's [*Performance Data*](https://www.apcprop.com/technical-information/performance-data/) (undated webpage, accessed 2026) explicitly describes its output as a vortex-theory computation using propeller geometry. The direct [*12×8E Performance Data*, version v2022-0915, simulation dated 2022-09-22](https://www.apcprop.com/files/PER3_12x8E.dat), includes a 10,000-RPM table. The repository's electric-Stik installation uses selected rows of that predicted table, as documented in `research/propulsion/stik-6s/README.md` and the generated aircraft data. Comparing it with UIUC at matched `J` is useful for assessing model sensitivity, but different RPM and blade Reynolds number confound the comparison; it is **not** an experimentally measured error bound at 10,000 RPM. Neither table proves the assembled propulsion system's transient or shutdown behavior.

APC's [*12×8E product specification*](https://www.apcprop.com/product/12x8e/) identifies this product as Thin Electric. Its [*RPM Limits*](https://www.apcprop.com/technical-information/rpm-limits/) page (undated, accessed 2026) gives a suggested limit of 150,000 divided by diameter in inches for that family, or 12,500 RPM for the nominal 12-inch part. Treat this as a manufacturer operating constraint to check in a real test plan, not as evidence that the simulated installation enforces protection or is safe throughout its envelope. Confirm the actual propeller variant and its condition.

**Motor and glow-engine catalog evidence.** The [Scorpion *SII-4020-540 Motor Propeller Data*](https://www.scorpionsystem.com/files/i2,653_data_chart.htm) (undated chart, accessed 2026) lists a 12×8E point at 22.2 V, 49.92 A and 10,334 RPM. It is useful as an external installation-level comparison, with its measurement conditions and voltage reference requiring confirmation. The project used chart points in a conditional motor fit; those same points cannot simultaneously serve as independent validation of that fit. Reserve other operating conditions and, ideally, measurements of the actual installation for validation.

O.S. Engines' [*MAX-61FX, Code No. 17750*](https://www.os-engines.co.jp/english/line_up/engine/air/aircraft/catalog/17750.htm) (undated catalog page, accessed 2026) specifies 1.9 PS at 16,000 RPM, a practical range and engine mass. A rated power at one RPM does not identify torque over the loaded operating range, installed propeller thrust, fuel use or throttle transients. The basic Stik's prescribed-RPM representation and its propeller analog therefore need their own installation evidence; the electric 12×8E data should not be silently reused for its nominal glow propeller.

**Full-envelope modeling is a method, not a Jensen coefficient set.** Selig's [*Real-Time Flight Simulation of Highly Maneuverable Unmanned Aerial Vehicles*](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/pubs/Selig-FSOne-AIAA-Journal-of-Aircraft.pdf), *Journal of Aircraft* 51(6), 1705–1725 (2014), [DOI 10.2514/1.C032370](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.C032370), develops a component-based six-degree-of-freedom method for highly maneuverable small aircraft, including large angles and propeller-wash effects. It motivates investigating local flow, surface geometry, propulsion coupling and the validity of a low-angle approximation. The paper's illustrated configurations and modeling choices are not measurements of the Jensen. A more elaborate model is a research candidate; it is not automatically more accurate for this aircraft.

**Low-Reynolds-number airfoils.** UIUC's [*Low-Speed Airfoil Tests*](https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/uiuc_lsat.html) identifies a body of measured section data; for example, Selig and colleagues' *Summary of Low-Speed Airfoil Data*, Volume 1 (1995), covers Reynolds numbers from 30,000 to 500,000. These results help select plausible sensitivity cases and test techniques. A traced or visually similar section is not identification of the real built, covered wing's profile, transition or roughness, and a two-dimensional section polar is not the complete aircraft's finite-wing, tail and fuselage response.

**FASER is an analog, not the same Stik.** Hoe, Owens and Denham, [*Forced Oscillation Wind Tunnel Testing for FASER Flight Research Aircraft*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120014506) (2012), measured roll- and yaw-axis dynamic behavior over angle-of-attack, sideslip, rate and frequency conditions; the authors discuss increased dynamic dependence near and beyond stall. Their [paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120014506/downloads/20120014506.pdf) identifies a Hangar 9 Ultra Stick 120 airframe. Owens, Cox and Morelli, [*Development of a Low-Cost Sub-Scale Aircraft for Flight Research: The FASER Project*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20060022541) (2006), describe a valuable stronger design: the **same FASER test article** was used in wind-tunnel and flight work, with instrumentation and data-compatibility checks. Same-article means same FASER, not the 60-inch Jensen. FASER's geometry, powertrain, mass properties and measurement conditions differ. Its curves are a plausibility challenge and test-design example, not target Jensen derivatives; verify the reported oscillation/reduction conventions before comparing individual coefficients.

**Flight-test identification.** Morelli, Cunningham and Hill, [*Global Aerodynamic Modeling for Stall/Upset Recovery Training Using Efficient Piloted Flight Test Techniques*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140002791) (2013), show how informative, sufficiently independent excitations and held-out maneuvers can improve model identification. Their reported demonstration used a fixed-base transport-aircraft simulator and a wind-tunnel-derived aerodynamic database. It demonstrates a method against a known simulated source, not real-world validation of the Jensen or a ready-made RC flight-test script. Begin with simpler, controlled maneuvers appropriate to the actual aircraft and flight-test environment.

## Measurement and validation sequence

**1. Freeze identity and establish the instrumented aircraft.** Record exact simulator commit, engine/backend, aircraft and configuration files, generator versions and hashes, scene, weather, start condition and input profile. Describe the physical build by airframe and wing version, engine/motor, propeller variant, ESC and firmware, pack or fuel, servo/linkage arrangement, gear and repair state. Photograph and measure the actual covered wing, tails, incidence, thrust line and surface geometry; mark uncertainty and distinguish planned dimensions from measured dimensions. Use the repository's existing measurement reducers and generated-data pipeline rather than directly editing generated output.

Measure ready-to-fly mass and three-dimensional CG at relevant fuel states or with the actual installed battery, not just catalog component weights. Measure moments of inertia about roll, pitch and yaw on the assembled configuration, with fixture/tare correction, repeated periods and uncertainty; estimate cross terms where the chosen apparatus permits. Record the actual datum, axes, units and sign conventions. Repeat after material configuration changes. These measurements constrain trim and rotational response without pretending that a numerically convenient balance weight is physical ballast.

**2. Characterize input before changing aircraft physics.** With the intended radio/controller, firmware and OS, record raw hardware events, the calibrated channels, rates/expo and transmitter trims, simulator rate selection, post-trim commands and actual surface angle. Measure both endpoints, midpoint, asymmetry, hysteresis and return, and the response to repeatable steps and reversals. Include loaded servo/linkage travel where practicable. Characterize throttle separately, including the gamepad's virtual throttle where used. Check connection, focus loss, recovery and held-at-start behavior on real devices, not only injected callbacks. Synchronize clocks or document offsets between input capture, onboard telemetry, high-speed surface video and displayed output; report acquisition and sample rates, calibration, measurement uncertainty and missing channels. Do not attribute a perceived delay entirely to the actuator before measuring controller, frame and display latency.

**3. Measure propulsion and local flow independently.** Identify and instrument the actual propeller and source installation. For the electric setup, record pack state, temperature, terminal and measurement-point voltage, current, shaft RPM and thrust across steady operating points and repeatable transients; characterize torque if equipment permits. Check coast/brake settings and forward-flow conditions separately from static thrust. Compare `CT` and `CP` with the proper measured diameter, speed, density and RPM; retain manufacturer and UIUC tables with source version and uncertainty. The manufacturer's motor-chart points used for fitting are training evidence, not held-out acceptance points. For glow, record fuel, prop, mixture and loaded RPM/thrust response separately; a catalog peak does not determine an installed torque curve. Use a qualified test facility and operating procedures appropriate to the propeller's limits.

To investigate propwash, compare powered and lower-power cases with matched airspeed, surface deflection and measured RPM/thrust, and measure or bound local tail flow and immersed surface geometry where feasible. Separate axial thrust, direct propeller torque/gyroscopic effects and the tail's aerodynamic increment. A zero-airspeed bench observation alone cannot identify full-flight wake contraction or the correct coupling model. Record negative or inconclusive results as evidence.

**4. Validate software, components and aircraft in that order.** Start with schema/units, conservation and sign checks, baseline exact replay, fixed-step and substep convergence, bounded inputs, contact/restart behavior, and native/reference parity within the declared domain. Keep solver and model identity in every result. Then compare measured mass/CG/inertia, surface dynamics and propulsion with their corresponding component predictions, including uncertainties and held-out operating points. Finally compare complete-aircraft motion: matched trim at several airspeeds and power settings; small pitch/roll/yaw inputs and reversals; power changes; glide and recovery; runway acceleration and landing contact. Expand toward slow flight, high-angle behavior and spins only when the aircraft, instrumentation and test range permit. Evaluate signs, time histories, peak and steady response, damping and uncertainty together; a single matching trajectory is insufficient.

Before fitting, partition data by independent flights or conditions, withhold entire maneuvers and configurations rather than random adjacent ticks, and write down expected domains and acceptance bands based on measurement uncertainty and repeatability. Avoid fitting a compensating aerodynamic term to an unknown CG, servo lag or motor error. Change one model assumption at a time, run the same recorded input tape and report effects across both fitted and withheld cases. Use FASER and UIUC as bounded comparisons; label extrapolation explicitly and retain cases where a candidate worsens another response.

**5. Evaluate perceived feel and actual delivery separately.** Ask experienced RC pilots to fly structured tasks using the same controller mapping and documented trim, start, weather, camera, display and audio settings; collect individual ratings and comments on response, predictability and workload. Compare simulator candidates in randomized, blinded or masked A/B order where possible; counterbalance order and repeat tasks to reduce learning effects. Physical-aircraft versus simulator comparison cannot be fully blinded, so label it and align conditions as closely as practical. Correlate pilot observations with the recorded post-rate inputs, servo positions, rates, airspeed and latency, rather than treating preference as a direct aerodynamic measurement.

Then qualify the actual native packages and hardware: controller and OS matrix, timing at the primary 1920×1080 target, frame-time distribution, simulated-time versus elapsed-time behavior and audio/camera presentation. Native Mac admission and a GDScript/native replay establish only their named domains; laptop or headless results do not qualify all GPUs, controllers or resolutions. Record exact source, dependencies and the complete checked verdicts.

The completed audit includes eleven three-case static aircraft cards, four eight-case dynamic aircraft cards and a separate eight-case finite-pack card, four baseline golden trajectories, fourteen Python runner regressions and four expected direct-CLI rejections. These are scoped software results, not measurements of a physical aircraft or pilot acceptance. The [verification report](verification.md) records the final identities, retained receipts, additional focused checks and environmental failures.
